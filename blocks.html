The craft

Pattern, pressed by hand.

Block printing is one of the simplest and most beautiful ideas in textiles. A pattern is carved into a block of wood, the block is dipped in colour, and then it is pressed onto cloth by hand. Press, lift, move along, press again. The pattern grows across the fabric one impression at a time.

Because a person makes every press, no two are identical. Those small differences are the quiet signature of a handmade cloth.

Every print comes out a little different.

From wood to cloth

How a block is made.

Five steps, one patient pair of hands. Scroll to follow a block from raw wood to printed cotton.

01 · Wood

Step 01

It begins with solid wood

A block starts as a piece of solid wood, cut to size and smoothed flat, ready to hold a pattern for years of pressing.

Step 02

A pattern is drawn

The motif is sketched onto the wood. Every line is deliberate, because every line will be cut by hand.

Step 03

Carved by hand

A carver cuts away everything that should stay blank, leaving the pattern standing proud. A single block can take up to a week.

Step 04

Dipped in colour

The raised pattern is pressed onto a pad of colour, so that only the carved lines pick up the ink.

Patterns with more colours use one block per colour.

Step 05

Pressed onto cloth

The block is pressed firmly onto cotton, lifted, moved along and pressed again. Every impression is made by a person, so every one has its own character.

The collection

Meet the blocks.

Tap a block to open it: see it from every side and find out what its pattern traditionally stands for.

The meanings here are how each motif is traditionally read. They are an introduction to the tradition, and the carvers may tell these stories in their own way.

Star & Medallion Lattice

A repeating net of stars and round medallions. This kind of geometric all-over pattern is often seen in Ajrakh printing from Kutch and Sindh.

Traditionally read as

Stars and rosettes are often read as sky and flower: the night sky and a garden in one pattern.

Photo 1 of 2

Mandala Rosette

A circular mandala at the centre, framed by paisleys and petals. 'Mandala' is Sanskrit for circle.

Traditionally read as

A circle around a centre is traditionally read as wholeness, with everything held in balance.

Square Spiral Grid

Interlocking square spirals that run into each other like a maze, in the family of 'key' or meander patterns found in many cultures.

Traditionally read as

Spirals and meanders are often read as a path or journey with no beginning and no end.

Blooming Vine Jaal

Flowers on a winding creeper. 'Jaal' means net or lattice: a pattern that can repeat and spread across a whole cloth.

Traditionally read as

The creeper is a classic Indian sign of growth, abundance and life continuing.

Peacock Feather Column

A tall column of feather or lotus-petal shapes, stacked and mirrored. It reads as peacock feathers or lotus petals, or both.

Traditionally read as

The peacock is India's national bird and stands for beauty, joy and the monsoon. The lotus stands for purity: it rises clean from the mud.

Photo 1 of 2

Grand Paisley

A big paisley filled with flowers and leaves. The shape comes from the Persian and Kashmiri 'boteh'. The English name comes from Paisley in Scotland, where shawls with this motif were woven in the 1800s.

Traditionally read as

In India it is often called the mango motif (ambi or kairi) and read as abundance and fertility.

Photo 1 of 5

Sun Burst

A sun with a ring of rays around a dotted centre. Sun motifs appear all over Indian art and architecture.

Traditionally read as

The sun is life and energy: the thing that makes everything grow.

Photo 1 of 5

Spiral

A single spiral inside a toothed ring. It is one of the oldest patterns people have drawn, and it appears in shells, ferns and galaxies.

Traditionally read as

A spiral is often read as growth, a cycle, or a journey outward from the centre.

Photo 1 of 4

Fern Leaf

A long leaf with feathery side veins, drawn like a fern frond.

Traditionally read as

Leaves stand for new growth. One leaf is easy to print in a row, a circle or a pile.

Photo 1 of 4

Endless Knot

A knot with no beginning and no end. It is one of the Eight Auspicious Symbols (Ashtamangala) of Buddhism.

Traditionally read as

It is read as everything being connected: wisdom, compassion and life all tied together.

Photo 1 of 5

Striped Leaf

A broad leaf with straight, regular veins. This is the simplest and boldest of the leaf blocks.

Traditionally read as

Like the fern, it is a plain sign of growth and nature. Printed in rows it makes a field of leaves.

Photo 1 of 4

Candy-cane Tree

A little festive tree with a candy cane and ornaments, a modern motif made for the Christmas market.

Traditionally read as

Indian carvers also make modern festive designs for the Christmas market. A fun reminder that block printing keeps moving with the times.

Photo 1 of 4

Festive Tree with Gifts

A Christmas tree with a star on top, baubles and a wrapped present at the base. Another modern festive block.

Traditionally read as

It is made for the Christmas market, which is why it sits next to far older motifs in our collection.

Photo 1 of 4

Little Paisley (Buti)

A small paisley, a 'buti' (small motif). Printed many times, it builds up a pattern of its own.

Traditionally read as

Like its big sister, it is often read as the mango motif: abundance, and luck.

Photo 1 of 3

Zigzag & Scallop Border

A zigzag line with a scalloped edge. Borders (kinara in Hindi) frame the cloth and give it a finished edge.

Traditionally read as

Zigzags are often read as water or hills, and a border says: the picture is complete.

Photo 1 of 3

Hills & Waves Border

Triangles filled with wave lines, like hills with water between them.

Traditionally read as

Hills and water appear again and again in border designs, and are often read as a landscape in miniature.

Photo 1 of 3

Double Wave Border

Two rows of wave curls that mirror each other.

Traditionally read as

Waves are often read as a river or the flow of time, and it is a border you can print in one long unbroken line.

Photo 1 of 3

Spiral Wave Border

A row of spiral curls joined like waves.

Traditionally read as

It is a close cousin of the spiral block, and another sign of motion and growth.

Photo 1 of 3

Creeper Border

A narrow vine with curling tendrils running along the block.

Traditionally read as

The creeper is a classic border: life, growth and continuity along the edge of a cloth.
