From their hands

Every piece passes through many hands.

Before a Ghar piece reaches your home, it passes through about six pairs of hands in India. This page is about them.

The journey

From a drawing to your table.

  1. 1

    The drawing

    Every print starts on paper: a flower, a triangle, a trellis, drawn and redrawn until it repeats without a seam.

  2. 2

    The block

    A carver cuts the design into a block of wood with small chisels, by hand. One block can take up to a week.

  3. 3

    The print

    The printer dips the block in colour and presses it onto the cotton, again and again, lining up every repeat by eye. Each colour needs its own block.

  4. 4

    The colour

    The cloth is washed, dried in the sun and set, so the colour stays bright wash after wash.

  5. 5

    The stitch

    A tailor cuts and sews the cloth into a cushion, a runner or a pouch, and finishes every edge.

  6. 6

    The check

    Someone checks every piece, folds it, and packs it for the long journey to Amsterdam.

The story of

Delftpur

Delftpur is our blue and white. Its blue triangles sit somewhere between India's blue cities and the blue-and-white tiles of Delft: two homes, one colour.

The triangle block is small, so each piece is printed row after row, every triangle pressed and lined up by hand. Look closely and you will see the tiny shifts that tell you a person made it.

The story of

Gulabi

Gulabi means rosy pink. It is a soft rose-and-leaf pattern in peachy pinks and garden greens, the print we reach for when a room needs warmth.

Two colours means at least two blocks and two rounds of printing, the second laid exactly over the first by eye.

See the Gulabi pieces

The story of

Haldi

Haldi is turmeric, the warm yellow of every Indian kitchen. A pink trellis scattered with orange rosebuds, finished with a turmeric-yellow edge.

It is our smallest batch so far: just a few runners, mats and cushions, each one printed and edged by hand.

See the Haldi pieces

The story of

Ikat

With ikat, the pattern lives in the thread itself. Before weaving, bundles of thread are tied tightly and dyed, again and again, so each thread already carries its part of the design.

Then the weaver lines them up on the loom, and the pattern appears slowly as the cloth is woven. The soft, feathered edges are the mark of the hands that made it.

See the Ikat pieces
